\subsection{Raw, Uncompressed Storage Favours Native UTM}
\input{tables/table_storage_accounting}

Table~\ref{tab:storage_accounting} gives the raw storage accounting. The
HEALPix level-20 tile and the equal-area \term{UTM crop} are both fixed-size
representations of the same nominal ground area, so their raw byte
counts are geometric constants. At $\gamma_{\mathrm{tile}}=1$ the HEALPix tile is
$2.584\times$ larger than the crop, exactly $1024^2/637^2$ on every region
(its range over the 40 regions is $2.584$ to $2.584$). It matches the
analytic factor $\alpha_{\mathrm{HPX/native}}\approx2.58$ of
\eqref{eq:alpha} because the crop side was derived from it: this ratio
is fixed by geometry and identical for every region. Under the illustrative
$\gamma_{\mathrm{tile}}=1.30$ scenario the uncompressed tile is still
$1.99\times$ the size of the crop scaled by $\gamma_{\mathrm{tile}}$: a
$30\%$ overhead cannot offset a $2.58\times$ oversampling. The three
rows compare float32 rasters, the form the codecs sweep. The native
product is distributed as 16-bit integers; stored in that form the crop
is half the size again, so the tile is $5.17\times$ the product as it
ships (last row). The sweeps stay in float32. The audit of
Appendix~\ref{app:codec_audit} shows this is not the decisive choice:
the image codecs are indifferent to the input type within $0.3\%$,
lossless JPEG~XL is $34\%$ smaller on integer digital numbers, and
converting the \term{carrier} to those integers costs it a quantisation of
$2.9\times10^{-5}$ that the native product never pays.
Only compression, by exploiting the redundancy that oversampling
represents, can make HEALPix competitive.
