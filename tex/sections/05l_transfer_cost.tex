\subsection{What the Transfer Costs to Store}
\label{sec:transfer_cost}
This section prices the \term{transfer} itself. The three \term{transfers}
do not serve the same purpose. Clough--Tocher keeps the resolution and
the instrument response of the product. The PSF-aware \term{transfer} is
designed to restore small-scale structure, so its tile carries content
the native raster does not. The inverse-distance average over the four
nearest cells of Section~\ref{sec:resampler} (the library's
\texttt{linear} kernel) restores none and serves as the control: without
it, every property of a deconvolving \term{transfer} would be confounded
with its not being Clough--Tocher. The protocol is run on tiles rebuilt
from the same native windows, on 40 regions for the PSF \term{transfer}
and 39 for the inverse-distance average. The PSF tiles are built at the
fixed damping of the companion's synthetic recovery experiments
($\lambda=10^{-3}$) and at the nominal \SI{12.5}{m} response width. The
conversion chain of Appendix~\ref{app:large_scale_trial} uses
$\lambda=0.1$; how the surcharge moves with the damping has not been
measured.

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} uses short names: C--T for Clough--Tocher, PSF
for the PSF-aware \term{transfer}, IDW for the inverse-distance average.
Every ratio is a paired median over the regions where the codec the
oracle selects brackets the \term{target} on the three \term{carriers}
and on the \term{native crop}; $n$ falls to 30 at the loosest
\term{target}. Block (a) gives each \term{carrier} against the
\term{native crop} with no overhead, and its compressed size relative to
the Clough--Tocher tile at the same absolute RMSE. Block (b) gives each
raster's lossless size and, at each \term{target}, its compressed size as
a fraction of that lossless size.

The control is informative. At the same coding RMSE, each measured
against its own uncompressed \term{carrier}, the deconvolved tile costs
$1.26$ to $2.45$ times the Clough--Tocher tile, increasing with the
\term{target}, and the inverse-distance average costs $1.20$ to $2.15$.
Relative to each other the two exceed parity by only $1.03$ to $1.14$,
separable at the three tightest \term{targets} and not at the two
loosest. This PSF/IDW differential of $3$--$14\%$ suggests that
deconvolution alone is unlikely to explain the roughly two-fold
surcharge, since the \term{transfer} that restores no detail pays nearly
all of it too. The two \term{transfers} differ in kernel, smoothness and
behaviour under noise, so the experiment does not isolate the
contribution of deconvolution; that would need a non-deconvolving
\term{transfer} as smooth as Clough--Tocher.

Block (b) separates content from codability. The lossless sizes are
within $9\%$ of each other: \SI{1.31}{MB}, \SI{1.37}{MB} and \SI{1.43}{MB} for Clough--Tocher, PSF and inverse-distance. The slopes
differ: at the $10^{-2}$ \term{target} the Clough--Tocher tile
compresses to $1.1\%$ of its own lossless size, the deconvolved tile to
$2.6\%$, the inverse-distance tile to $3.3\%$ and the native raster to
$3.8\%$. Scene complexity does not explain the surcharge: correlating
each region's lossless size with the surcharge it pays gives a Spearman
$\rho$ of $-0.12$ for the PSF \term{transfer} ($p=0.456$, $n=40$) and
$-0.08$ for the inverse-distance average ($p=0.615$, $n=39$). The
deconvolution puts power back into the small scales, $2.80$ times the
observation's in the top octave against $0.89$ for Clough--Tocher, and a
coder held to an RMSE must encode it. The inverse-distance average adds
no such power ($0.92$) and still pays: against an analytic field its
output lies twice as far from the truth as that of Clough--Tocher,
$16.20\%$ against $7.97\%$ of the field's standard deviation. Content
that is neither the scene nor suppressed must be coded as content. The
comparison of five kernels on accuracy, spectrum and size is in the data
deposit.

The consequence for the headline result is one of the largest
sensitivities measured in this study. Under Clough--Tocher the HEALPix
\term{carrier} is the cheaper representation at the two loosest
\term{targets} ($0.94$ and $0.72$ against the native product under the
illustrative overhead). Under either other \term{transfer} it is cheaper
at none: $2.00$ and $1.78$ for the PSF \term{transfer}, $1.88$ and $1.57$ for the inverse-distance average. With no overhead, the
PSF-aware tile compresses to between $2.62$ and $2.32$ times the
\term{native square}, and the inverse-distance tile to between $2.49$
and $2.05$ times; both barely move with the tolerated error, while the
content of the Clough--Tocher tile is mostly the smooth redundancy a
codec is built to remove.

Nothing in this section measures the quality of the PSF-aware
\term{transfer}; it establishes the storage price of the content that
\term{transfer} adds. The companion study reports that the matched
reconstruction recovers $15.4\%$ to $19.6\%$ of an imposed blur on a
known noise-free texture at that damping, and, with damping and
iterations selected by cross-validation, a small accuracy advantage over
tuned deconvolutions on real reduced-resolution Sentinel-2 imagery;
against an independent higher-resolution \term{reference}, no method it tests
improves on bicubic interpolation~\cite{delouis2026resample}. Our
synthetic harness reproduces that recovery without noise, and loses
most of it at the noise level measured on the imagery, both at
$\lambda=10^{-3}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:error_budget}). On these tiles the
surcharge therefore prices content whose recovery is not established
here. Among the \term{transfers} that reproduce the product,
Clough--Tocher is both the most accurate and the most compressible: a
\term{transfer} that represents a band-limited field faithfully does not
invent fine-scale content, and content it does not invent is content
the codec need not pay for.
