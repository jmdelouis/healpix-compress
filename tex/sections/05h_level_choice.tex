\subsection{What Level 20 Buys Over Level 19}
\label{sec:level_choice}

Section~\ref{sec:redundancy} chose level 20 on a nominal argument. The
object measured here is a stored level-20 tile aggregated in groups of
four, which in the NESTED ordering are adjacent elements and exactly the
four children of one level-19 cell. Because the cells are equal-area,
this is the exact quadtree Haar transform of the tile, and the aggregate
is the cell-average of the \term{carrier} over each level-19 cell. It differs
from the \term{carrier} the same \term{transfer} would build at level 19, which stores
point evaluations at cell centres. Both were built and read identically
against the same observation, on the 40 regions, all of which pass the
window audit of Appendix~\ref{app:window_radiometry}. They are different arrays,
differing by $6.3\%$ of a scene's standard deviation. Yet their floors
agree to a median ratio of $1.002$ and cost $2.25$ and $2.24$ times the
level-20 floor. What follows therefore holds for either reading of ``the
level-19 \term{carrier}''. Appendix~\ref{app:level19_direct} gives the
comparison and the one region it excludes. The figure quoted below is
the aggregate's.

Two quantities are separated, because a coarser \term{carrier} is charged twice
and only one charge concerns information. The first is what the
four-child aggregation discards: the intra-cell variance, the energy of
the three detail coefficients of the quadtree Haar transform at level 20.
It is $3.47\%$ of the variance of the tile (median over 40 regions;
$2.7\%$ in agriculture to $7.2\%$ in urban scenes), an RMS of $18.6\%$ of the
scene's standard deviation, which is $1.82$ times the level-20 floor of
Table~\ref{tab:floors}. The second is the floor of the
aggregate itself, read the same way against the same observation: $2.24$
times the level-20 floor (paired median over the 40 regions). The two
separate cleanly. That floor equals the level-20 floor and the discarded
energy added in quadrature to within $7\%$ (median ratio of measured to
quadrature $1.07$), and the discarded energy alone accounts for $84\%$
of the excess variance. The extra error of the aggregate is thus the
information the aggregation removed. $66.9\%$ of the sampled operating
points in the extended codec sweeps lie below that floor, a share that
depends on how far the ladders reach. A cell-averaged level-19 \term{carrier}
already carries more error than most of the settings this study swept,
and in that sense level 19 cannot replace level 20 here.

The result also cautions against the spectral argument that pointed the
other way. The observed field carries $0.21\%$
of its variance above half the native Nyquist frequency
(Section~\ref{sec:support_controls}). Propagating the fitted spectrum
to the level-19 Nyquist frequency places about $0.03\%$ above it, two
orders of magnitude below what the aggregation discards. Aggregation is
no brick-wall filter at the coarser Nyquist frequency. The residual of a
$2\times2$ mean has response $1-\cos^2(k_x d/2)\cos^2(k_y d/2)$. It
grows as $(kd)^4$ and draws mainly on the shoulder of the spectrum
\emph{below} the native Nyquist frequency, where the power lies. The same
fitted spectra predict the discarded fraction well only in the ensemble
median (predicted $3.4\%$ against $3.1\%$ measured), one more reason to
report the spectral model as an ensemble parameter only.

What the coarser \term{carrier} costs to store is measured as well
(Table~\ref{tab:level19_rd}). The direct level-19 \term{carrier} and its
cell-averaged twin are run through the codec sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:rd_protocol} and compared with the \term{native crop} and the
level-20 tile under the paper's rules. Raw, the level-19 \term{carrier} is
$0.65$ times the \term{native crop}. Compressed to equal error it stays smaller
than the crop at every \term{target}, on $89\%$ to $100\%$ of the regions. Its
ratio rises from $0.67$ at the tightest \term{target} to $0.78$ at the loosest.
Compression recovers nothing for a \term{carrier} that has already discarded
samples; at loose \term{targets} it erodes the raw advantage instead.
Against the level-20 tile the same \term{carrier} costs $0.32$ of the bytes at
the tightest \term{target} and $0.87$ at the loosest, so the level-20 tile
costs $1.15$ times the level-19 \term{carrier} at $10^{-2}$ and $3.1$ times at
$10^{-4}$. The cost of the finer \term{carrier} is the cost of its density,
largest where the codec keeps every sample and nearly gone where it
keeps few. The cell-averaged \term{carrier} is $9\%$ cheaper than the direct
one at equal error (median over $200$ region--\term{target} pairs), the price
of the sharper point evaluations the \term{transfer} keeps, and it is cheaper
on every region at the four tightest \term{targets}. Its advantage grows with
the \term{target}, from $0.95$ to $0.83$ of the direct \term{carrier}'s size. This is
why its column against the \term{native crop} is flat where the direct
\term{carrier}'s rises: at the loosest \term{target} the aggregate compresses to
$0.9\%$ of its own raw size, against $0.8\%$ for the \term{native crop} and
$1.2\%$ for the direct \term{carrier}, so the aggregate and the crop gain
compressibility at nearly the same rate as the \term{target} loosens. A
$2\times2$ mean of a Clough--Tocher tile behaves, per stored value, much
like the native raster it came from; the directly evaluated \term{carrier}
keeps the sharper point values and pays for them.
