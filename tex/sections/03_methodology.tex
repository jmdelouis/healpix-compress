\subsection{From a Nested Chunk to a 2-D Raster}
\label{sec:morton}
Every codec used here is a generic 2-D array or image compressor with no
knowledge of HEALPix, so a chunk must first become an ordinary raster in
which horizontal and vertical neighbours are spatial neighbours. A nested
chunk of $4^{\delta}$ cells (all descendants, $\delta$ levels down, of one
parent) is a 1-D array indexed by a Morton (Z-order) code: bit $b$ of the
local $x$ coordinate sits at bit $2b$ of the index, bit $b$ of $y$ at bit
$2b+1$. De-interleaving the bits recovers $(x,y)$ for every cell and
scatters the array into a $2^{\delta}\times2^{\delta}$ raster, exactly
invertibly. We use $\delta=10$: the $1024\times1024$ level-20 descendants
of one level-10 parent form one carrier tile, large enough to amortise
per-chunk codec overhead and small enough to stay a practical storage
unit. This reshaping is the only HEALPix-specific step; all later steps
work on a plain float32 raster. That raster still carries three marks of
its origin: the kernel that filled it from the sensor grid and the shape
anisotropy of equal-area HEALPix cells (a local axis-length ratio of up
to $\sim\!1.7$ between the raster axes), which both affect
compressibility (Section~\ref{sec:resampler}), and its axes, which in
the UTM zone of a scene run at about $45^\circ$ to north
(Fig.~\ref{fig:utm_crop_footprints}), so the row and column predictors
of the codecs follow diagonals of the native raster; what that
orientation costs or saves has not been measured
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Equal-Area Native UTM Comparison}
\label{sec:utm_crop}
Both sides must cover the same nominal area, and a carrier tile
projected into the UTM zone of a scene is a rotated square. An
axis-aligned bounding box around it holds about twice the pixel count of
the true footprint and would penalise the native side. Each grid is
therefore compared in its own geometry, matched by ground area. HEALPix is equal-area, so the ground area of a level-20 tile is a
geometric constant: by \eqref{eq:healpix_width}, a $1024\times1024$-cell
tile covers a square of side $1024\,\Delta_{\mathrm{eq}}(20)\approx\SI{6.37}{km}$,
about $636.7$ native \SI{10}{m} pixels, rounded to $637$. For each scene a
$637\times637$-pixel axis-aligned square is cut from the native Sentinel-2
B04 raster, centred on the native pixel nearest to the mean longitude and
latitude of the tile's cells, computed with \texttt{healpix\_geo} on the
WGS84 ellipsoid. The square extends beyond the rotated tile along its
edges and falls short at its corners; ``equal-area'' is shorthand for equal
\emph{nominal projected} area. UTM is conformal, so $637^2$ nominal pixels
do not enclose exactly the geodesic area of the tile, and rounding $636.7$
to $637$ alone adds about $0.1\%$ of area before the zone's scale factor;
these terms are small next to the $2.58\times$ oversampling under study
and have not been computed scene by scene. Both representations have a
fixed raw size across regions by construction, \SI{1.623}{MB} for the
$637\times637$ native crop ($637^2\times\SI{4}{B}$) and \SI{4.194}{MB}
for the $1024\times1024$ HEALPix tile, with no fill value on either side.

Matching by area means each grid is compressed in the frame it is stored
in, with no resampling of the native side, the comparison a practitioner
faces. It also means the two representations encode different ground
over part of their extent: over the 40 regions the intersection is
$74$--$83\%$ of the crop ($81\%$ median), an intersection-over-union of
about $0.68$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:utm_crop_footprints}), and supports that
differ over a fifth of their extent can have different entropies.
Section~\ref{sec:support_controls} bounds the effect with an analytic
field that both geometries sample identically; that control reproduces
the decline of the measured gap to within $5$--$24\%$, and the crossover
itself once the field passes through the transfer. The mismatch matters
more for cross-grid comparisons of reconstruction \emph{error}, which is
why the common-reference evaluation of
Section~\ref{sec:common_reference} is restricted to the intersection.

\subsection{The Resampling Kernel Is Part of the Result}
\label{sec:resampler}
Filling the level-20 tile means resampling the native raster onto the
HEALPix cell centres, a step with no counterpart on the UTM side. During
validation, the 2-D power spectrum of the resulting raster showed excess
high-frequency power relative to the native spectrum of the same
footprint and to the tangent-plane spectra of the companion
study~\cite{delouis2026resample}; left in place, this excess would have
lowered the apparent compressibility of HEALPix. A smooth gradient in
cell placement cannot inject high frequencies, so positional distortion
was ruled out. Cell anisotropy contributes without sufficing: at fixed
area the two local sample spacings scale as $\sqrt{1.7}$ and
$1/\sqrt{1.7}$, which brings the shorter spacing closer to the native
\SI{10}{m} pitch than the nominal \SI{6.2}{m} scale suggests.

The cause we could attribute directly was the resampler. The tiles were
first filled with a Shepard-type inverse-distance interpolator over the
four nearest neighbours, with a kernel width set to the HEALPix
\emph{output} scale ($\sim\SI{6.2}{m}$) instead of the \SI{10}{m}
\emph{input} spacing. The 40-region ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:support_controls} later qualified the first impression
that it injected high-frequency content: in the radially averaged
spectrum the inverse-distance transfer leaves no excess power in the top
octave ($0.92$ of the observation's), while it is $9.5$ times less
accurate than Clough--Tocher against an analytic field and its output
costs about twice the bytes at equal error
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}). Kernels are compared with the
approximation-order framework of~\cite{blu1999fourier} and the kernel
comparison of~\cite{thevenaz2000interpolation}, by scoring each against
an analytic field. The replacement is a Clough--Tocher piecewise-cubic
interpolant over a Delaunay triangulation, distinct from bicubic
interpolation (\texttt{scipy.interpolate.griddata} with
\texttt{method="cubic"}). It brought the HEALPix spectrum much closer to
the native one, and all results of Section~\ref{sec:results} use it.
The sensitivity to this choice is measured: Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}
rebuilds the carrier with the PSF-aware and inverse-distance transfers
and repeats the sweep on the 40 regions, and
Section~\ref{sec:support_controls} scores five kernels against an
analytic field on the 39 regions the window audit of
Appendix~\ref{app:window_radiometry} admits. The crossover reported
below is a property of the carrier built with this transfer. The lesson
reaches beyond this paper: the kernel that fills a HEALPix grid from a
sensor grid is a scientific choice, and a kernel mismatched to the source
sampling can change which of two grids looks more storage-efficient once
compressed.

The PSF-aware transfer is described here so that this paper stands on its
own. It models each native pixel as a Gaussian-weighted sum of the latent
field at the level-20 cell centres, with the instrument's response as the
weight (nominal B04 width \SI{12.5}{m} FWHM), assembles that forward
operator as a sparse matrix from cells to samples, and estimates the cell
values by damped least squares, solved by conjugate gradients on the
normal equations with a damping of $\lambda=10^{-3}$, the value at which
the companion study reports its accuracy. Its estimand is therefore the
latent field before the instrument blur; the companion study measures
that it recovers $15.4\%$ to $19.6\%$ of the blur on noise-free fields,
and Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost} prices the bytes this sharper
estimand costs. The PSF-aware transfer and the forward operator of
Section~\ref{sec:operator} come from the \texttt{healpix-resample}
package at its tag \texttt{jstars-submission}, the version the companion
paper describes; the environment record written with every sweep names
the checkout it ran against, because the package's PSF width convention
changed once between versions during this work.

\subsection{Codecs Compared}
\label{sec:codecs}
No custom or HEALPix-aware codec is used. We evaluate established,
independently maintained codecs, so that any redundancy that survives
compression can be exploited with tools a practitioner can already
install. Learned codecs are absent by design: they have reshaped the
rate--distortion frontier for natural images since~\cite{balle2017endtoend}
and reached remote sensing, including on-board use~\cite{valsesia2019onboard},
but their performance depends on the training distribution, and no pair
of models trained symmetrically on the two grids exists. Compression
practice for optical space imagery is surveyed
in~\cite{blanes2014tutorial}; the mission-side standards there, the CCSDS
wavelet coder and its multispectral successor~\cite{hernandez2021ccsds123},
target the on-board bandwidth problem, where complexity, memory and channel
errors constrain the coder as much as its rate~\cite{abrardo2010onboard},
while the question here is archive-side. Five general-purpose compressors
are applied identically on both grids:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ZFP}~\cite{lindstrom2014zfp}: a fixed-accuracy compressed floating-point array codec based on a near-orthogonal block transform;
    \item \textbf{SZ3}~\cite{liang2022sz3}: a modular, prediction-based, error-bounded lossy compressor for scientific floating-point data;
    \item \textbf{JPEG~2000}~\cite{taubman2000jpeg2000}, lossy and lossless: a wavelet codec with embedded block coding and optimised truncation (EBCOT);
    \item \textbf{JPEG~XL}~\cite{alakuijala2019jpegxl}, lossy and lossless: a multiscale (\emph{Squeeze}) modular mode for lossless and near-lossless coding and a block-DCT (\emph{VarDCT}) mode for lossy coding;
    \item \textbf{DWT+Zstandard}: a textbook baseline, a multi-level 2-D wavelet transform~\cite{antonini1992wavelet} with uniform scalar quantisation, entropy-coded with Zstandard~\cite{rfc8878zstd}.
\end{itemize}
Each codec is swept over its own quality parameter (tolerance for ZFP,
absolute error bound for SZ3, quality/rate for JPEG~2000, distance/level
for JPEG~XL, quantisation depth for DWT+Zstandard) to trace a full
rate--distortion curve; JPEG~2000 and JPEG~XL add one lossless point each.
Each ladder runs from near-lossless to settings coarse enough that the
loosest target of Section~\ref{sec:rd_protocol} is exceeded on every
region whose contrast permits it (ZFP tolerance up to $3$, SZ3 error bound
up to $1$, JPEG~2000 compression ratio up to $4000$, JPEG~XL down to its
coarsest quality level and largest Butteraugli distance, DWT+Zstandard
down to two quantisation bits): 71 operating points per region and grid.
Sweeping the parameter bypasses each codec's rate control on purpose (in
JPEG~2000 the quality layers and the post-compression optimisation that
fills them, a research subject in itself~\cite{auli2008scalability}): a
rate controller targets a budget, and this comparison needs an
\emph{achieved} error or size on both grids, so the matching is done
afterwards (Section~\ref{sec:rd_protocol}).

\subsection{Codec Preference Is Empirical}
\label{sec:why_quadtree}
The $2\times2$ block structure of the de-interleaved raster aligns with
HEALPix parent--child groups, and the experiments here do not isolate an
advantage from that alignment. A native UTM raster supports the same
dyadic transforms, and Section~\ref{sec:results} finds the same shift
towards JPEG~2000 and JPEG~XL at aggressive ratios on \emph{both} grids.
The codec ranking is therefore an empirical finding about these codecs on
this imagery; a direct test of a quadtree-native coder
(Appendix~\ref{app:quadtree}) found no advantage large enough to matter.
