\section{Carrier-Grid Compression Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{From a Nested Chunk to a 2-D Raster}
\label{sec:morton}
Every codec used in this paper is a generic two-dimensional array or image
compressor with no knowledge of HEALPix. To apply such a codec to a HEALPix
chunk, the chunk must first be converted into an ordinary 2-D raster in which
horizontally and vertically adjacent elements are spatial neighbours. A
nested chunk of $4^{\delta}$ cells (all descendants, $\delta$ levels down, of
one coarser parent) is a one-dimensional array whose index is a Morton
(Z-order) code: bit $b$ of the local $x$ coordinate occupies bit $2b$ of the
index and bit $b$ of the local $y$ coordinate occupies bit $2b+1$.
De-interleaving the bits recovers $(x,y)$ for every cell and scatters the
flat array into a $2^{\delta}\times2^{\delta}$ raster; the operation is
exactly invertible. We use $\delta=10$ throughout, so that the $1024\times1024$
level-20 descendants of one level-10 parent form one carrier tile, which is
large enough to amortise per-chunk codec overhead and small enough to remain
a practical unit of chunked storage.

This reshaping is the only HEALPix-specific step of the compression
pipeline; all subsequent steps operate on an ordinary float32 raster. The
raster reaching the codec is nevertheless not unremarkable. Both the kernel
used to populate the grid from the native sensor grid and the local shape
anisotropy of equal-area HEALPix cells (equal in area but not in shape, with
a local axis-length ratio of up to $\sim\!1.7$ between the two raster axes)
affect its compressibility (Section~\ref{sec:resampler}). The reshaping is
geometrically neutral; the sampling that precedes it is not.

\subsection{Equal-Area Native UTM Comparison}
\label{sec:utm_crop}
A fair comparison requires both sides to cover the same amount of ground,
but the two grids are not co-oriented. A carrier tile projected into the UTM
system of a scene is a rotated square, because the local axes of HEALPix do
not align with UTM north and east. An axis-aligned bounding box around the
tile contains roughly twice the pixel count of its true footprint and would
penalise the native side; this was observed on real scenes before the
protocol below was adopted.

Each grid is instead compared in its own native geometry, matched by ground
area rather than by footprint. Because HEALPix is equal-area, the ground
area of a level-20 tile is a geometric constant. By \eqref{eq:healpix_width},
a $1024\times1024$-cell tile covers a square of side
$1024\,\Delta_{\mathrm{eq}}(20)\approx\SI{6.37}{km}$, i.e.\ approximately
$636.7$, rounded to $637$, native \SI{10}{m} pixels per side. For each scene
a $637\times637$-pixel axis-aligned square is therefore extracted from the
native Sentinel-2 B04 raster, centred on the native pixel nearest to the mean
longitude and latitude of the cells of the tile, computed with
\texttt{healpix\_geo} on the WGS84 ellipsoid. The square is not
pixel-for-pixel identical to the rotated tile: it extends beyond the tile
along its edges and falls short of its corners. It is called equal-area
throughout as a shorthand for equal \emph{nominal projected} area. UTM is
conformal rather than equivalent, so $637^2$ nominal pixels do not enclose
exactly the geodesic area of the tile, and rounding $636.7$ to $637$ alone
adds about $0.1\%$ of area before the scale factor of the zone is
considered. These terms are small compared with the $2.58\times$
oversampling under study, but they are not zero, and they have not been
computed scene by scene. What the two sides share by construction is the raw
size, $637^2\times\SI{4}{B}\approx\SI{1.62}{MB}$, constant across the 40
regions in the same way as the fixed HEALPix tile, with no fill value on
either side. Figure~\ref{fig:utm_crop_footprints} shows both footprints for a
sample of regions.

Matching by area rather than by shape means that each grid is compressed in
the frame in which it is stored, with no resampling of the native side,
which is the comparison a practitioner faces. It does not mean that the two
representations encode the same ground. Raw byte counts are unaffected by
the mismatch, but compressed size depends on texture, edges and class
boundaries, so two supports of equal area that differ over a fifth of their
extent can have different entropies. Over the 40 regions the intersection is
$74$--$83\%$ of the crop ($81\%$ median), an intersection-over-union of
about $0.68$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:utm_crop_footprints}); the mismatch is not
negligible. Section~\ref{sec:support_controls} bounds its contribution by
replacing the scene with an analytic field that both geometries sample
identically. That control reproduces the monotonic decline of the measured
gap and its approximate magnitude, to within $8$--$27\%$; the residual
cannot be uniquely attributed to the mismatch, to scene content or to the
transfer, but sampling geometry alone suffices to produce the effect. The
mismatch bears more
directly on cross-grid comparisons of reconstruction \emph{error}, which is
why the common-reference evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:common_reference} is
restricted to the intersection.

\subsection{Resampling Kernel Choice Is Not Neutral}
\label{sec:resampler}
Populating the level-20 tile requires resampling the native raster onto the
HEALPix cell centres, a step with no counterpart on the UTM side. During
validation, the 2-D power spectrum of the resulting raster showed visibly
excess high-frequency power relative to the native spectrum of the same
footprint and to the tangent-plane spectra of the companion
study~\cite{delouis2026resample}. Left uncorrected, this excess would have
degraded the apparent compressibility of HEALPix. Slow positional distortion
of the grid was ruled out as a cause, since a smooth gradient in cell
placement cannot inject high frequencies. Cell anisotropy is a genuine but
insufficient contributor: at fixed area the two local sample spacings scale
as $\sqrt{1.7}$ and $1/\sqrt{1.7}$, which brings the shorter spacing closer
to the native \SI{10}{m} pitch than the nominal \SI{6.2}{m} level-20 scale
suggests. We describe the effect as an interpolation artefact rather than as
aliasing in the strict sampling-theory sense.

The cause that could be attributed directly was the resampler. The tiles
were originally populated with a Shepard-type inverse-distance interpolator
over the four nearest neighbours, whose kernel width was set to the HEALPix
\emph{output} scale ($\sim\SI{6.2}{m}$) rather than to the \SI{10}{m}
\emph{input} spacing, and a visual comparison of spectra suggested that it
injected high-frequency content. The 40-region ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:support_controls} later qualified this impression: in the
radially averaged spectrum the inverse-distance transfer leaves no excess
power in the top octave ($0.92$ of the observation's), but it is $9.5$ times
less accurate than Clough--Tocher against an analytic field, and its output
costs about twice the bytes at equal error (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}).
The replacement was justified; the reason first given for it was only partly
so. Comparing kernels quantitatively rather than visually is a well-posed
problem, for which the approximation-order framework of~\cite{blu1999fourier}
and the kernel-by-kernel comparison of~\cite{thevenaz2000interpolation}
provide the tools; Section~\ref{sec:support_controls} applies them by
scoring each kernel against an analytic field rather than against the
imagery it was derived from. Replacing the resampler with
\texttt{scipy.interpolate.griddata(method="cubic")}, a Clough--Tocher
piecewise-cubic interpolant over a Delaunay triangulation (not bicubic
interpolation), brought the HEALPix spectrum much closer to the native one,
and all results of Section~\ref{sec:results} use it. The sensitivity to this
choice is measured rather than assumed: Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}
rebuilds the carrier with the PSF-aware and the inverse-distance transfers
and repeats the sweep, and Section~\ref{sec:support_controls} scores five
kernels against an analytic field, the first on the 40 regions and the
second on the 39 the window audit of
Appendix~\ref{app:window_radiometry} admits. The reader should
be aware of their outcome in advance: the crossover reported below is a
property of the carrier built with this transfer and does not survive the
other two. The implication for reproducibility extends beyond this paper.
The kernel used to populate a HEALPix grid from a native sensor grid is not
a neutral implementation choice, and a kernel mismatched to the source
sampling can change which of two grids appears more storage-efficient once
compressed.

\subsection{Codecs Compared}
\label{sec:codecs}
No custom or HEALPix-aware codec is used. We evaluate established,
independently maintained codecs so that any redundancy found to survive
compression can be exploited with tools a practitioner can already install.
One family is deliberately absent. Learned codecs have reshaped the
rate--distortion frontier for natural images since~\cite{balle2017endtoend}
and have been applied to remote sensing, including on-board
scenarios~\cite{valsesia2019onboard}. They are not evaluated here because
the performance of a learned codec depends on its training distribution, so
that a HEALPix-versus-UTM comparison would measure the training set as much
as the geometry, and no pair of models trained symmetrically on the two
grids exists. Compression practice for optical space imagery is well
surveyed in~\cite{blanes2014tutorial}. The mission-side standards described
there, the CCSDS wavelet coder and its multispectral
successor~\cite{hernandez2021ccsds123}, target the on-board bandwidth
problem, where complexity, memory and channel errors constrain the coder as
much as its rate~\cite{abrardo2010onboard}, rather than the archive-side
question studied here. Five general-purpose compressors are used, applied
identically on both grids:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ZFP}~\cite{lindstrom2014zfp}: a fixed-accuracy compressed floating-point array codec based on a near-orthogonal block transform;
    \item \textbf{SZ3}~\cite{liang2022sz3}: a modular, prediction-based, error-bounded lossy compressor for scientific floating-point data;
    \item \textbf{JPEG~2000}~\cite{taubman2000jpeg2000}, lossy and lossless: a wavelet codec with embedded block coding and optimised truncation (EBCOT);
    \item \textbf{JPEG~XL}~\cite{alakuijala2019jpegxl}, lossy and lossless: a multiscale (\emph{Squeeze}) modular mode for lossless and near-lossless coding and a block-DCT (\emph{VarDCT}) mode for lossy coding;
    \item \textbf{DWT+Zstandard}: a textbook baseline consisting of a multi-level 2-D wavelet transform~\cite{antonini1992wavelet} with uniform scalar quantisation, entropy-coded with Zstandard~\cite{rfc8878zstd}.
\end{itemize}
Each codec is swept over its own native quality parameter (tolerance for
ZFP, absolute error bound for SZ3, quality/rate for JPEG~2000, distance/level
for JPEG~XL, quantisation depth for DWT+Zstandard) to trace a full
rate--distortion curve; JPEG~2000 and JPEG~XL contribute one lossless point
each. Each ladder runs from the near-lossless end to settings coarse enough
that the loosest target of Section~\ref{sec:rd_protocol} is exceeded on
every region whose contrast permits it (ZFP tolerance up to $3$, SZ3 error
bound up to $1$, JPEG~2000 compression ratio up to $4000$, JPEG~XL down to
its coarsest quality level and largest Butteraugli distance, DWT+Zstandard
down to two quantisation bits), for a total of 71 operating points per
region and grid. Sweeping the parameter deliberately bypasses the rate control of each
codec, in JPEG~2000 the quality layers and the post-compression optimisation
that populates them, which are a research subject in their own
right~\cite{auli2008scalability}. A rate controller targets a budget,
whereas this comparison requires an \emph{achieved} error or size on both
grids; the matching is therefore performed afterwards
(Section~\ref{sec:rd_protocol}).

\subsection{Codec Preference Is Empirical, Not a Demonstrated Quadtree Effect}
\label{sec:why_quadtree}
Although the $2\times2$ block structure of the de-interleaved raster aligns
with HEALPix parent--child groups, the experiments reported here do not
isolate an advantage from that alignment. A native UTM raster supports the
same dyadic transforms, and Section~\ref{sec:results} finds the same shift
towards JPEG~2000 and JPEG~XL at aggressive ratios on \emph{both} grids. The
codec ranking is therefore treated as an empirical finding about these
codecs on this imagery, not as evidence of a quadtree advantage; a direct
test of a quadtree-native coder (Appendix~\ref{app:quadtree}) found none
large enough to matter.
