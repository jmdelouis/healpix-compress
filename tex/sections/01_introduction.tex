\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\IEEEPARstart{E}{arth} observation archives grow in volume, combine several
sensors and resolutions, and move to the cloud. Their use depends on a
storage layout with a global spatial reference, fast local access and
scalable chunking. Discrete Global Grid Systems (DGGS) divide the Earth into
hierarchically indexed cells that serve as a shared reference for storage,
aggregation and analysis~\cite{ogc_dggs,iso19170}. H3~\cite{h3},
S2~\cite{s2geometry} and HEALPix~\cite{gorski2005healpix} differ in cell
shape, refinement rule and area preservation. Cloud-native products move
in this direction: chunked array stores such as Zarr~\cite{moore2023zarr},
community tooling for DGGS data cubes such as
\texttt{xdggs}~\cite{kmoch2024xdggs}, and ESA's Earth Observation
Processing Framework, which republishes the Sentinel archive as Zarr.

HEALPix was designed for astronomical data on the
sphere~\cite{gorski2005healpix}. Its cells have equal area and refine
dyadically: whatever the ordering, every cell contains exactly four
cells of the next level. The nested index adds a storage property to
this geometry: it places all descendants of a cell in one contiguous
block. That is what lets a tile be compressed on its own. The ring
ordering would instead force compression by whole rings of latitude.
These properties suit Earth observation as well, with one
practical limit: HEALPix resolution changes only by powers of two, so
the native sampling of an instrument almost never matches a level. The
\SI{10}{m} bands of Sentinel-2~\cite{drusch2012sentinel2} fall between
level 19 (\SI{12.4}{m}, too coarse) and level 20 (\SI{6.2}{m},
geometrically safe and nominally oversampled). This paper asks whether
that oversampling is expensive once storage and compression are both
counted. Level-20 samples are not independent observations at
\SI{6.2}{m}; they form an overcomplete representation whose information
content the sensor response still limits. If this redundancy compresses
well, storing the data at level 20 could cost no more than keeping the
same information on the native UTM grid. The native product has a cost
of its own. Under an operational tiling layout such as the MGRS granules
of Sentinel-2 it carries padding, overlap and per-tile metadata beyond
the area needed. A survey of that grid finds that it enlarges the stored
land area by $33\%$, about one petabyte per year of
products~\cite{bauer2023wasting}. This paper uses a $30\%$ overhead as
an illustrative value at the level of that spatial overhead
(Section~\ref{sec:overhead}).

The answer, in a data manager's terms, is given in
Section~\ref{sec:headline}. Before compression, a level-20 tile takes $2.58$
times the bytes of the area-matched native raster. After compression to
the same reconstruction error, it takes $2.06$ times as many when the
tolerated error is a quarter of a percent of a typical scene's contrast.
It takes $0.93$ times as many, a bare crossing, marginal in significance
(Section~\ref{sec:rd_main}), when the tolerated error is a quarter of
that contrast. These ratios compare each raster with its own uncompressed
version. Scored against the Sentinel-2 observation instead
(Section~\ref{sec:common_reference}), native UTM leads at every
reportable storage budget, while the size advantage at the loosest error
survives under an interpolating \term{read}\footnote{Terms in italics are defined in
Table~\ref{tab:glossary}, Section~\ref{sec:glossary}.}.

Two \term{transfers} can fill the
tile, and they answer different questions.
The main results use Clough--Tocher
interpolation~\cite{clough1965plate}, which passes a smooth surface
through the observed pixels and reads it at the HEALPix cell centres. It
keeps the resolution of the product, so the tile and the native raster
carry the same information at the same instrument response, and the
comparison isolates what the grid costs in compressed bytes. The
PSF-aware \term{transfer} of the companion study~\cite{delouis2026resample}
also undoes part of the instrument blur and aims at a sharper latent
field. Its tile carries small-scale content that the native raster does
not, by design, and costs $2.3$--$2.6$ times the native raster at every
error. Those bytes pay for content the product gains, whose benefit that
study establishes, and this paper prices them without ranking the two
\term{transfers} against each other (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}). The
choice of \term{transfer} is therefore one of the largest sensitivities in the
storage cost of a HEALPix \term{carrier}.
Section~\ref{sec:glossary} defines the nine terms the paper relies on.

The question is answered empirically, with established compression
libraries and no HEALPix-specific coding. The finer HEALPix level is
treated as a plain 2-D raster, obtained by Morton (Z-order) de-interleaving
of the nested chunk, and passed to standard codecs as an ordinary image.
The comparison covers matched ground area: the centroid of the HEALPix
tile defines an axis-aligned, \term{equal-area square} cut from the native UTM
raster (Section~\ref{sec:utm_crop}), so the UTM side is always the
untouched native product. The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a storage--distortion formulation of the resolution mismatch
    between native Sentinel-2 sampling and the next finer HEALPix level,
    with a geometric accounting: the $2.58\times$ oversampling of level 20
    makes the raw HEALPix tile the \emph{larger} representation by
    $\approx\!1.99$ under the illustrative $30\%$ overhead, so only
    compression can make HEALPix competitive;
    \item an equal-area rate--distortion protocol that separates the
    overhead-free \term{carrier}/native \term{size ratio} from the overhead of the native
    layout through a critical-overhead $\gamma_\star$ analysis, at matched
    error and \term{matched budget}, against a \term{native UTM square} of the same
    nominal area;
    \item an evaluation over 40 Sentinel-2 regions, four land-cover
    classes, latitudes from $43.5^\circ$S to $64.1^\circ$N (both HEALPix
    cell-shape regimes) and five general-purpose codecs. It includes
    sensitivity analyses on codec, \term{target}, \term{transfer} and assumed overhead,
    and a multiple-testing correction that seven of the eight per-\term{target}
    comparisons survive. Two controls explain the result. An
    analytic-field control shows that sampling geometry alone reproduces
    the decline of the gap with the \term{target}. A smoothed-native control
    shows that transfer-induced smoothing is sufficient to account for a
    residual of the observed magnitude
    (Section~\ref{sec:support_controls});
    \item a reusable, assertion-guarded pipeline that shares its sites,
    products and resampling operator with the companion
    study~\cite{delouis2026resample}, so the storage and \term{transfer} questions
    are answered on the same data.
\end{itemize}

The competitive regime reported below belongs to the complete chain.
It depends on four quantities: the overhead of the native product,
carried as $\gamma_\star(e)$ so that no value is assumed; the \term{transfer}
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}); the codec
(Section~\ref{sec:same_codec}); and the \term{reconstruction reference}
(Section~\ref{sec:common_reference}). These four conditions belong with
every statement of the form ``HEALPix is smaller''. The comparison
compresses each tile, band and date alone, as the native product is
stored. It therefore does not count what a shared grid adds: coding
across dates, bands and sensors that sit on the same cells, and chunks
shaped to the read pattern that the nested index makes contiguous.
That gain is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:shared_grid}; the ratios
here are the floor it starts from.
