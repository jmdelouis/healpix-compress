\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\IEEEPARstart{E}{arth} observation archives are growing in volume, combine
several sensors and resolutions, and are increasingly hosted in the cloud.
Their scientific use depends on storage organisations that provide globally
consistent spatial referencing, efficient local access and scalable
chunking. Discrete Global Grid Systems (DGGS) divide the Earth into
hierarchically indexed cells that serve as a shared spatial reference for
storage, aggregation and analysis~\cite{ogc_dggs,iso19170}. Prominent
instances include H3~\cite{h3}, S2~\cite{s2geometry} and
HEALPix~\cite{gorski2005healpix}, which differ in cell shape, refinement
strategy and area preservation.

HEALPix was designed for the analysis of astronomical data on the
sphere~\cite{gorski2005healpix}. It provides equal-area pixels, a dyadic
hierarchical refinement and a nested indexing scheme in which all descendants
of a pixel occupy a contiguous block of indices. These properties are equally
attractive for Earth observation, but they impose a practical limitation:
HEALPix resolution changes only by powers of two, so the native ground
sampling distance of an instrument almost never coincides with a level. The
\SI{10}{m} bands of Sentinel-2, for example, lie between level 19
(\SI{12.4}{m}, too coarse) and level 20 (\SI{6.2}{m}, geometrically safe but
nominally oversampled).

The question addressed in this paper is whether such oversampling is
expensive once storage and compression are both considered. The level-20
samples are not independent observations at \SI{6.2}{m}; they form an
overcomplete representation whose information content remains limited by the
sensor response. If this redundancy compresses well, storing the data at
level 20 and compressing them should cost no more, and possibly less, than
keeping the equivalent information on the native UTM grid. The native
product, when distributed under an operational tiling layout such as the
MGRS granule scheme of Sentinel-2, carries padding, overlap and per-tile
metadata beyond the area actually needed. This paper uses a $30\%$ overhead
as an illustrative operational scenario; it is not a property of UTM as a
coordinate reference system (Section~\ref{sec:overhead}).

The answer, in the terms a data manager would use, is given in one table
and one figure (Table~\ref{tab:headline} and Fig.~\ref{fig:headline},
Section~\ref{sec:headline}). Before compression, a level-20 tile takes
$2.58$ times the bytes of the native raster covering the same ground.
After compression to the same reconstruction error, it takes $2.06$ times
as many when the tolerated error is a quarter of a percent of the contrast
of a typical scene, and $1.06$ times as many when the tolerated error is a
quarter of that contrast; the land-cover class of the scene moves these
figures by less than a fifth. That is the outcome for a tile filled by
Clough--Tocher interpolation, which passes a smooth surface through the
observed pixels and reads it at the HEALPix cell centres. Filling the same
tile with the PSF-aware transfer of the companion
study~\cite{delouis2026resample}, which also undoes part of the blur of
the instrument, produces a sharper product whose compressed size stays at
$2.3$--$2.7$ times the native raster at every error, that is, the
uncompressed factor is not recovered at all. The choice of transfer is
thus the largest single factor in the storage cost of a HEALPix carrier,
larger than the codec, the scene or the tolerated error, and the paper
gives it the space that this implies. Readers who are not specialists in
compression or signal processing will find the few terms the paper relies
on, and the reading of each, in Table~\ref{tab:glossary}.

The question is addressed empirically, with established and independently
maintained compression libraries and without any HEALPix-specific coding.
The finer HEALPix level is treated strictly as a two-dimensional raster,
obtained by Morton/Z-order de-interleaving of the nested chunk, and passed to
standard codecs as a conventional image. The comparison is made over matched
ground area: the centroid of the HEALPix tile defines an axis-aligned,
equal-area square extracted directly from the native UTM raster
(Section~\ref{sec:utm_crop}), so that the UTM side is always the untouched
native product.

The contributions of this work are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a storage--distortion formulation of the resolution mismatch between native Sentinel-2 sampling and the next finer HEALPix level, with a geometric accounting showing that the $2.58\times$ oversampling of level 20 makes the raw HEALPix tile the \emph{larger} representation by a factor of $\approx\!1.99$ under the illustrative $30\%$ granule-overhead scenario, so that compression rather than raw storage must make HEALPix competitive;
    \item an equal-area rate--distortion protocol that separates the intrinsic compressibility of the grids from the overhead of the native layout through a critical-overhead $\gamma_\star$ analysis, comparing HEALPix tiles with a native UTM square of identical nominal area (rather than a bounding box of the rotated footprint, which inflates the UTM pixel count by roughly $2\times$), with matched error and matched budget obtained by log--log interpolation over the sweep of each codec;
    \item an evaluation across 40 Sentinel-2 regions, covering four land-cover classes and latitudes from $43.5^\circ$S to $64.1^\circ$N, hence both HEALPix cell-shape regimes (Section~\ref{sec:latitude}), and five general-purpose codecs, with sensitivity analyses on codec, target, resampling operator and assumed overhead, a multiple-testing correction that seven of the eight per-target comparisons survive, and a control that replaces the scene by an analytic field with the spectrum of the imagery and shows that support mismatch and scene content together account for less than a fifth of the effect (Section~\ref{sec:support_controls});
    \item a reusable, assertion-guarded evaluation pipeline that shares its sites, products and resampling operator with a companion study of HEALPix resampling~\cite{delouis2026resample}, so that the storage and transfer questions are answered on the same data.
\end{itemize}

The scope of the result should be stated before the result itself. The
competitive regime reported below belongs to the complete carrier-grid chain
(transfer, carrier and read), not to HEALPix geometry alone, and it is
conditional on three quantities, each measured here rather than assumed. The
first is the storage overhead assigned to the native product, which is never
fixed: every verdict is expressed through the critical overhead
$\gamma_\star(e)$ of Section~\ref{sec:rd_main}, and the $30\%$ scenario is
one abscissa on that curve. The second is the transfer that populates the
carrier: the same tiles rebuilt with a PSF-aware or an inverse-distance
transfer cost $1.2$ to $2.2$ times the bytes at equal accuracy and leave
HEALPix ahead at no target (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}), a sensitivity
larger than the advantage itself. The third is the reconstruction reference:
when the carrier is scored against the native observation rather than within
its own representation, its transfer-and-read floor of about $10\%$ of a
scene's standard deviation is not reached by any swept configuration under
the nearest-cell read; two thirds of the variance of that floor is due to the
reading operator, and re-scoring through an interpolating read narrows the
lead of native UTM without reversing it (Sections~\ref{sec:operator}
and~\ref{sec:common_reference}). These three conditions are stated together
here so that no statement of the form ``HEALPix is smaller'' is read without
them.
