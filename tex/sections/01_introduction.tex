\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\IEEEPARstart{E}{arth} observation archives grow in volume, combine several
sensors and resolutions, and move to the cloud. Their use depends on a
storage layout with a global spatial reference, fast local access and
scalable chunking. Discrete Global Grid Systems (DGGS) divide the Earth into
hierarchically indexed cells that serve as a shared reference for storage,
aggregation and analysis~\cite{ogc_dggs,iso19170}; H3~\cite{h3},
S2~\cite{s2geometry} and HEALPix~\cite{gorski2005healpix} differ in cell
shape, refinement rule and area preservation.

HEALPix was designed for astronomical data on the
sphere~\cite{gorski2005healpix}. Its cells have equal area, its refinement
is dyadic, and in its nested index all descendants of a cell form one
contiguous block. These properties suit Earth observation as well, with
one practical limit: HEALPix resolution changes only by powers of two, so
the native sampling of an instrument almost never matches a level. The
\SI{10}{m} bands of Sentinel-2 fall between level 19 (\SI{12.4}{m}, too
coarse) and level 20 (\SI{6.2}{m}, geometrically safe and nominally
oversampled).

This paper asks whether that oversampling is expensive once storage and
compression are both counted. Level-20 samples are not independent
observations at \SI{6.2}{m}; they form an overcomplete representation
whose information content the sensor response still limits. If this
redundancy compresses well, storing the data at level 20 could cost no
more than keeping the same information on the native UTM grid. The native
product has a cost of its own: under an operational tiling layout such as
the MGRS granules of Sentinel-2 it carries padding, overlap and per-tile
metadata beyond the area needed. This paper uses a $30\%$ overhead as an
illustrative scenario for one layout (Section~\ref{sec:overhead}).

The answer, in a data manager's terms, is in Table~\ref{tab:headline} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}. Before compression, a level-20 tile takes $2.58$
times the bytes of the area-matched native raster. After
compression to the same reconstruction error, it takes $2.06$ times as
many when the tolerated error is a quarter of a percent of a typical
scene's contrast, and $0.93$ times as many when it is a quarter of that
contrast. This holds for a tile filled by Clough--Tocher interpolation,
which passes a smooth surface through the observed pixels and reads it at
the HEALPix cell centres. The PSF-aware transfer of the companion
study~\cite{delouis2026resample}, which also undoes part of the instrument
blur, aims at a sharper latent-field estimand; its tile stays at
$2.3$--$2.6$ times the native raster at every error. The choice of
transfer is therefore the largest single factor in the storage cost of a
HEALPix carrier, above the codec, the scene and the tolerated error, and
the paper gives it the space this implies. Table~\ref{tab:glossary}
defines the few terms the paper relies on.

The question is answered empirically, with established compression
libraries and no HEALPix-specific coding. The finer HEALPix level is
treated as a plain 2-D raster, obtained by Morton (Z-order) de-interleaving
of the nested chunk, and passed to standard codecs as an ordinary image.
The comparison covers matched ground area: the centroid of the HEALPix
tile defines an axis-aligned, equal-area square cut from the native UTM
raster (Section~\ref{sec:utm_crop}), so the UTM side is always the
untouched native product.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a storage--distortion formulation of the resolution mismatch
    between native Sentinel-2 sampling and the next finer HEALPix level,
    with a geometric accounting: the $2.58\times$ oversampling of level 20
    makes the raw HEALPix tile the \emph{larger} representation by
    $\approx\!1.99$ under the illustrative $30\%$ overhead, so only
    compression can make HEALPix competitive;
    \item an equal-area rate--distortion protocol that separates the
    overhead-free carrier/native size ratio from the overhead of the native
    layout through a critical-overhead $\gamma_\star$ analysis, at matched
    error and matched budget obtained by log--log interpolation over each
    codec's sweep, against a native UTM square of the same nominal area (a
    bounding box of the rotated footprint would inflate the UTM pixel count
    by about $2\times$);
    \item an evaluation over 40 Sentinel-2 regions, four land-cover
    classes, latitudes from $43.5^\circ$S to $64.1^\circ$N (both HEALPix
    cell-shape regimes) and five general-purpose codecs, with sensitivity
    analyses on codec, target, resampling operator and assumed overhead, a
    multiple-testing correction that seven of the eight per-target
    comparisons survive, and an analytic-field control showing that
    sampling geometry alone reproduces the decline of the gap with the
    target and its approximate magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:support_controls});
    \item a reusable, assertion-guarded pipeline that shares its sites,
    products and resampling operator with the companion
    study~\cite{delouis2026resample}, so the storage and transfer questions
    are answered on the same data.
\end{itemize}

The scope comes before the result. The competitive regime reported below
belongs to the complete chain --- transfer, carrier, codec and read --- and
depends on four measured or parameterised quantities: the overhead of the
native product, expressed through $\gamma_\star(e)$ so that no value is
assumed; the transfer, which costs a factor of two
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}); the codec, since ZFP alone of the five
shows no crossover (Section~\ref{sec:same_codec}); and the reconstruction
reference, since against the native observation the transfer and the read
already cost about $10\%$ of a scene's standard deviation and native UTM
leads at every storage budget (Sections~\ref{sec:operator}
and~\ref{sec:common_reference}). These four conditions belong with every
statement of the form ``HEALPix is smaller''.
