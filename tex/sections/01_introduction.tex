\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\IEEEPARstart{E}{arth} observation archives grow in volume, combine several
sensors and resolutions, and move to the cloud. Their use depends on a
storage layout with a global spatial reference, fast local access and
scalable chunking. Discrete Global Grid Systems (DGGS) divide the Earth into
hierarchically indexed cells that serve as a shared reference for storage,
aggregation and analysis~\cite{ogc_dggs,iso19170}. H3~\cite{h3},
S2~\cite{s2geometry} and HEALPix~\cite{gorski2005healpix} differ in cell
shape, refinement rule and area preservation, and grids built for
high-resolution Earth observation weigh projection distortion against
oversampling~\cite{bauermarschallinger2014equi7}. Cloud-native products move
in this direction: chunked array stores such as Zarr~\cite{moore2023zarr},
DGGS data-cube tooling such as
\texttt{xdggs}~\cite{kmoch2024xdggs}, and ESA's Earth Observation
Processing Framework, which republishes the Sentinel archive as Zarr.

HEALPix was designed for astronomical data on the
sphere~\cite{gorski2005healpix}. Its cells have equal area and refine
dyadically: whatever the ordering, every cell contains exactly four
cells of the next level. The nested index adds a storage property to
this geometry: it places all descendants of a cell in one contiguous
block, so a tile can be compressed on its own. These properties suit
Earth observation as well, with one practical limit: in the nested
scheme, which carries that hierarchy, resolution changes only by powers
of two, so the native sampling of an instrument almost never matches a
level (the ring scheme accepts any $N_{\mathrm{side}}$, without the
parent--child index this study relies on). The \SI{10}{m} bands of
Sentinel-2~\cite{drusch2012sentinel2} fall between level 19
(\SI{12.4}{m}, coarser than the product) and level 20 (\SI{6.2}{m}, geometrically safe
and nominally oversampled). This paper asks whether that oversampling is
expensive once storage and compression are both counted. Level-20
samples form an overcomplete representation whose information content
the sensor response still limits; if this redundancy compresses well,
level 20 could cost no more than the native UTM grid. The native product
has a cost of its own: under the MGRS granules of Sentinel-2 it carries
padding, overlap and per-tile metadata, which a survey of that grid puts
at $33\%$ more stored land area, about one petabyte per year of
products~\cite{bauer2023wasting}.

Section~\ref{sec:headline} gives the answer. Before compression a
level-20 tile takes $2.58$ times the bytes of the area-matched native
raster, and $3.4$ times when both are coded losslessly after a 16-bit
requantisation. Coded with loss to the same error, an analytic field
sampled on both lattices, the one comparison in which both sides hold
the same content, falls from $2.17$ times the native size at the
tightest tolerance to $1.16$ at the loosest: oversampling is much
cheaper once compressed with loss, but not free. A tile filled by
Clough--Tocher interpolation~\cite{clough1965plate}, which keeps the
resolution of the product, falls from $2.07$ to $0.93$ against its own
native raster. That bare crossing is statistically marginal, is not
significant on the ground both grids share, comes from the smoothing of
the \term{transfer}, and lies at an error where coding alone changes
the NDVI class of $5$--$6\%$ of samples
(Sections~\ref{sec:rd_main} and~\ref{sec:support_controls}). A PSF-aware
\term{transfer}~\cite{delouis2026resample}, which also aims to undo part
of the instrument blur, costs $2.3$--$2.6$ times the native raster at
every error (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}): the choice of
\term{transfer} is one of the largest sensitivities in the storage cost
of a HEALPix \term{carrier}.

The question is answered empirically, with established compression
libraries and no HEALPix-specific coding. A nested chunk is
de-interleaved in Morton (Z-order) into a plain raster and passed to
standard codecs as an ordinary image, as HEALPix sky surveys store their
tiles~\cite{fernique2015hips}. The UTM side is an axis-aligned square of
the same nominal area cut from the native product, which is never
resampled (Section~\ref{sec:utm_crop}). The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an equal-area rate--distortion protocol that separates the
    overhead-free \term{size ratio} from the layout overhead of the
    native product through a \term{critical overhead} $\gamma_\star(e)$;
    \item an evaluation over 40 Sentinel-2 regions in four land-cover
    classes, from $43.5^\circ$S to $64.1^\circ$N, with five
    general-purpose codecs, three \term{transfers} and two
    \term{reconstruction references};
    \item two controls that explain the result: an analytic field shows
    that sampling geometry alone reproduces the decline of the ratio
    with the tolerated error, and a smoothed-native control shows that
    the smoothing of the \term{transfer} accounts for the crossing
    (Section~\ref{sec:support_controls});
    \item an assertion-guarded pipeline sharing sites, products
    and resampling operator with its companion~\cite{delouis2026resample}.
\end{itemize}

A statement of the form ``HEALPix is smaller'' therefore holds only
with four conditions: the overhead of the native product, carried as
$\gamma_\star(e)$; the \term{transfer}; the codec
(Section~\ref{sec:same_codec}); and the \term{reconstruction reference}.
Each tile, band and date is compressed alone, as the native product is
stored. What a shared grid adds, coding across sensors, zones and overlapping
tiles on the same cells, is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:shared_grid}; the
ratios here are the floor it starts from.
