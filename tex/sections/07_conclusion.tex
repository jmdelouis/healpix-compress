\section{Conclusion}
This paper asked whether Sentinel-2 imagery stored on the HEALPix level
next finer than its native resolution costs more to store once
compression and the layout of the native product are both counted. With
established codecs, no HEALPix-specific coding and an equal-area
protocol on 40 regions in four land-cover classes, the answer has four
parts.

\emph{Geometry.} Level 20 carries a fixed sampling-density penalty of
$2.584\times$ relative to the native \SI{10}{m} raster, which $30\%$ of
native-layout overhead reduces to $1.99\times$.

\emph{Compression.} Lossy coding removes part of that penalty at tight
tolerances and most of it at loose ones; lossless coding, after 16-bit requantisation, removes none,
and the tile then takes $3.42$ times the native bytes. With the same
content on both sides, an analytic field sampled on both lattices costs
$2.17$ to $1.16$ times the native size as the tolerated RMSE grows from
$10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$. The coarser level-19 \term{carrier} is smaller,
at $0.67$ to $0.78$ of the \term{native square}, but its transfer-and-read floor is about $2.25$ times that of level 20,
and against the observation it shows no saving over level 20.

\emph{Processing chain.} Against its own unsmoothed \term{native square}, the
Clough--Tocher tile falls from $2.07$ to $0.93$. The crossing comes from
the smoothing of the \term{transfer}: against a native raster read
through the same interpolant, the tile stays at $1.10$ to $1.24$ times
its size at the loosest \term{target}. It holds for four of the five
codecs but not for ZFP (Section~\ref{sec:same_codec}). A PSF-aware or an
inverse-distance \term{transfer} costs $1.2$ to $2.5$ times the bytes at
the same coding RMSE and leaves HEALPix ahead at no \term{target}
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}). The PSF/IDW differential is only
$3$--$14\%$, so deconvolution alone is unlikely to explain that
surcharge.

\emph{Reference.} Against the Sentinel-2 observation, native UTM leads
at all three reportable \term{matched-storage} budgets for the
Clough--Tocher tile, under both \term{reads}; the PSF-aware tile is not
scored, since its own forward operator reproduces the pixels it was
fitted to. Only the size advantage at
$10^{-2}$ survives, under the interpolating read and the
illustrative overhead (Section~\ref{sec:common_reference}).

Whether the \term{carrier} is the smaller archive is therefore a
comparison between the overhead the native product carries and
$\gamma_\star(e)$ at the distortion the archive tolerates. At $10^{-2}$
the threshold has fallen just below one, with a small and marginally
significant margin ($p=0.03$, $95\%$ confidence interval $0.89$--$0.99$)
on 32 of the 40 regions; at $10^{-4}$ it stands at $2.07$, an overhead we
know of no measurement supporting. The evidence is from Sentinel-2 B04
at \SI{10}{m}, one acquisition and one tile per region. The trial of
Appendix~\ref{app:large_scale_trial} shows a conversion chain on ten
whole products as it stood in October 2026, and fixes nothing about a
future production. Three measurements would sharpen the framework: the
compressed-byte overhead of a real tiled product; a non-deconvolving
\term{transfer} as smooth as Clough--Tocher; and the PSF-aware
\term{transfer} scored against the observation on pixels held out of
its fit. For the PSF-aware \term{transfer} the storage arithmetic points
to another design: keep the native pixels, whose halo around a tile adds
about $2\%$ rather than $30\%$, and run the \term{transfer} at read time
(Section~\ref{sec:grid_property}). The larger gain lies beyond this
framework. A shared grid puts every date, band and sensor on the same
cells; coding that coherence, first on global model output, where no
instrument response enters, starts from the floor measured here.
