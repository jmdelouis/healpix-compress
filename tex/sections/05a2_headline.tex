\subsection{The Compressed Size in One Table}
\label{sec:headline}

The rest of this section builds the comparison one factor at a time; its
outcome is in Table~\ref{tab:synthesis} and Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}. Both
start from one quantity: the compressed size of the HEALPix tile divided
by that of the area-matched \term{native UTM square}, at the same
reconstruction error, with no overhead charged to either side. The five
\term{targets} are those of Section~\ref{sec:rd_protocol}. Per \term{target}, the table gives that ratio for the
Clough--Tocher \term{carrier}, as the median over the regions, which is
also the \term{critical overhead} $\gamma_\star$
(Section~\ref{sec:rd_main}), and on the summed bytes of those regions
(Total); the share of the raw penalty that compression recovers; the
same \term{carrier} against a native raster read through the
\term{carrier}'s interpolant at half a pixel, its strongest smoothing
(HPX/sm., Section~\ref{sec:support_controls}); and the verdict over a
range of overheads and at the illustrative $30\%$. The first row is the smallest lossless file of each grid, lossless
after the 16-bit requantisation the image codecs receive
(Appendix~\ref{app:codec_audit}). $n$ counts the regions whose
sweeps bracket the \term{target} on both grids. Each grid is scored
against its own uncompressed raster; Section~\ref{sec:common_reference}
scores both against the observation. Under an overhead $\gamma$ on the
native product, every ratio at $\gamma=1$ divides by $\gamma$.

Three things follow. First, with the same content on both sides, lossy
coding removes part of the oversampling at tight tolerances and most of
it at loose ones, never all. Coded losslessly after that requantisation, which leaves an RMSE near
$6\times10^{-6}$ on both grids, the tile is $3.42$ times the square in median ($2.17$ to $6.92$ over the 40 regions): more than
the raw $2.58$, because the native values sit on the integer lattice of
the product and the interpolated ones do not. An analytic field sampled
on both lattices, where neither side is resampled, gives $2.17$ at
$10^{-4}$ and $1.16$ at $10^{-2}$, a quarter of the contrast of a
typical scene (Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}). It never
crosses parity.

Second, against its own \term{native square} the Clough--Tocher tile
goes further: $2.07$ at the tightest \term{target} and $0.93$ at the
loosest, a statistically marginal crossing that the ground both grids
share does not confirm (Section~\ref{sec:rd_main}). The difference from
the analytic curve is the smoothing of the \term{transfer}. Read through
the same interpolant, the native raster compresses better as well, and
at the loosest \term{target} the tile stays
$1.24$ times its size at a half-pixel phase and $1.10$ at a
quarter pixel (Section~\ref{sec:support_controls}). The tile pays for
that smoothing in error against the observation
(Section~\ref{sec:common_reference}); the crossing belongs to the chain,
not to the grid. The kind of scene moves the ratio less than the
tolerated error does: the class medians span $1.98$--$2.17$ at
the tightest \term{target} and $0.89$--$1.10$ at the loosest.

Third, the way the tile is filled is one of the largest sensitivities
of the result. The PSF-aware \term{transfer} of the companion
study~\cite{delouis2026resample} puts small-scale content on the tile,
and a codec asked to preserve it must spend bytes on it: the tile
compresses to $2.3$--$2.6$ times the \term{UTM square} at every
\term{target}, close to the uncompressed ratio. The inverse-distance
average, which restores no detail, costs nearly as much ($2.0$--$2.5$),
so the extra bytes are consistent with the price of leaving the smooth
interpolant (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_cost}). Under an illustrative
$30\%$ layout overhead on the native product every ratio divides by
$1.3$ (last block of Table~\ref{tab:synthesis},
Section~\ref{sec:rd_illustrative}).
